# Psychological Safety

## The Edmondson Model

Amy Edmondson's research (Harvard, 1999) established that psychological safety  -  the belief that one won't be punished for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes  -  is the strongest predictor of team performance.

### Google's Project Aristotle Findings
After studying 180+ teams, psychological safety was the #1 factor distinguishing high-performing teams. More important than:
- Team composition (who's on the team)
- Individual talent
- Workload
- Team size

### What It IS
- Safe to take interpersonal risks
- Safe to admit mistakes without blame
- Safe to ask "dumb" questions
- Safe to disagree with the majority
- Safe to propose unconventional ideas

### What It Is NOT
- Being nice all the time (conflict is healthy)
- Lowering standards (high standards + safety = learning zone)
- Avoiding accountability (safety enables honest accountability)
- Consensus requirement (disagree-and-commit still works)

## Measurement

### Team Survey (Edmondson's 7 Items, adapted)
Rate 1-5 (strongly disagree to strongly agree):

1. If I make a mistake on this team, it is NOT held against me
2. Team members can bring up problems and tough issues
3. People on this team do NOT reject others for being different
4. It is safe to take a risk on this team
5. It is NOT difficult to ask other team members for help
6. No one on this team would deliberately undermine my efforts
7. Working with this team, my unique skills and talents are valued

### Scoring
- Average >4.0: high safety
- Average 3.0-4.0: moderate (watch for specific weak items)
- Average <3.0: low safety (action needed urgently)

### Behavioral Indicators (Observable)
High safety signals:
- People speak up in meetings (not just seniors)
- Questions asked without hesitation
- Mistakes discussed openly in retros
- Ideas proposed that challenge status quo
- People say "I don't know" without embarrassment

Low safety signals:
- Silence in meetings (agreement by default)
- Blame language in incident reviews
- Knowledge hoarding
- People surprised by departures ("I had no idea they were unhappy")
- Ideas only flow top-down

## Improvement Actions

### Leader Behaviors (highest impact)

**Model vulnerability**:
- Share your own mistakes openly
- Say "I don't know" and "I was wrong"
- Ask for help publicly

**Respond productively to bad news**:
- When someone reports a problem: "Thank you for raising this"
- NEVER shoot the messenger (even if the news is bad)
- Ask: "What can we learn?" not "Whose fault is this?"

**Invite participation**:
- Ask quiet people directly: "What do you think, [name]?"
- Explicitly ask for disagreement: "What am I missing?"
- Create space: "Before we decide, what concerns haven't we discussed?"

### Team Practices

**Blameless postmortems**:
- Focus on systems and processes, not individuals
- "How did the system allow this failure?" not "Who caused this?"
- Action items improve systems, not punish people

**Retrospective quality**:
- Regular retros with varied formats
- Action items from retros actually get done
- Honest discussion of what's not working

**Learning celebrations**:
- "Failure Friday"  -  share what you learned from mistakes this week
- Document learnings, not just successes
- Reward speaking up about risks (even if nothing bad happened)

### Structural Changes

**Meeting design**:
- Silent brainstorming before discussion (prevents anchoring)
- Round-robin input (prevents domination)
- Anonymous input channels for sensitive topics

**Feedback systems**:
- Regular 1:1s with direct reports (not just performance review)
- Peer feedback normalized (not just manager → report)
- Separate growth conversations from evaluation conversations

## Engineering Culture Specifics

### Code Review Safety
- Reviews critique code, never people
- "What about..." instead of "This is wrong"
- Distinction: blocking issues vs suggestions vs nitpicks
- New team members' code reviewed constructively (mentoring, not gatekeeping)

### Incident Response
- No-blame culture in incident channels
- Post-incident timeline focuses on WHAT happened, not WHO
- Remediation items are systemic (add monitoring, add guard rails)
- People who cause incidents are THANKED for surfacing system weakness

### Technical Disagreements
- Encourage architecture discussions where junior voices count
- "Strong opinions, loosely held"  -  easy to change mind with evidence
- Document decisions and reasoning (ADRs) so disagreement is constructive
- Avoid HiPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion) deciding technical matters

### Experimentation Culture
- Failed experiments are learning, not waste
- Timeboxed experiments reduce fear of commitment
- "Let's try it for a week" reduces stakes of proposals
- Data settles disputes, not hierarchy

## Pitfalls

- Announcing "we have psychological safety" without measuring it
- Confusing safety with comfort (productive conflict requires discomfort)
- Leaders saying "my door is always open" but reacting poorly when used
- Survey results but no follow-up actions (worse than not surveying)
- Psychological safety for in-group only (watch for exclusion patterns)
- Treating safety as one-time initiative (requires continuous maintenance)
